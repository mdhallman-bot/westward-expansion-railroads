The Buffalo

How did buffalo skulls from the Great Plains end up at a factory in Michigan?

Rath & Wright's Buffalo Hide Yard, Dodge City, Kansas, mid-1870s. Buffalo hunter Charles Rath sits atop roughly 40,000 hides. Dodge City was a railhead on the Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway — hides were piled here specifically because the railroad could ship them east to market. (National Archives, public domain.)

Buffalo skulls piled at the Michigan Carbon Works, Rougeville, Michigan, 1892 — not on the Plains where the buffalo were killed. (Burton Historical Collection, Detroit Public Library, public domain.)
Point 1 — the man on top: He's standing on what was once tens of thousands of individual buffalo. Piles like this weighed hundreds of tons.
Point 2 — the bones themselves: These skulls were shipped by rail from the Great Plains all the way to Michigan — over a thousand miles from where the buffalo were killed.
Point 3 — the buildings in the background: This is the Michigan Carbon Works, a real bone-processing factory that turned skulls and bones into glue, fertilizer, and dye. The buffalo trade wasn't just hunting — it was a full industry with factories at the other end of the rail line.

Why buffalo were hunted so severely

• Commercial hide trade — hides sold in eastern markets for leather goods and industrial belting.

• A secondary bone trade — even the skeletons had value, shipped east by rail and processed into glue, fertilizer, and dye.

• Land clearing for cattle — open range was needed for the cattle trade the railroad had just made profitable.

• Military strategy — U.S. Army officers understood that destroying the buffalo would also destroy Plains nations' primary food, clothing, and shelter source, weakening their ability to resist confinement to reservations.
